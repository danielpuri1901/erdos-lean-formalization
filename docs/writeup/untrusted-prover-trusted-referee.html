Lean · AI agents · Claude Code

AI agents wrote computer-checked proofs of known math. They did not solve an open problem.

by Daniel Puri3 and 4 October 2026

I gave AI agents two kinds of math problems. The first kind was already solved on paper. The AI rewrote 26 of those results as proofs that a computer checks step by step, and a separate checker confirmed each one. The second kind was open: 16 problems that a public library marks as unsolved. The AI solved none of them. On two of them it proved new special cases.

The whole project used 244 USD of a 250 USD cloud credit.

What Lean is

Lean is a programming language for proofs.

  • You write the claim as code, and you write the proof as code.
  • A small trusted program, the kernel, checks every step of the proof.
  • If Lean accepts the proof, the claim is true. There is no room for a hidden mistake.

So "checking it" and "turning it into code" are the same thing here. Writing the proof as Lean code is how the computer checks it.

Where the problems came from

Google DeepMind keeps a library called formal-conjectures. It writes hundreds of math problems as Lean claims. Most of them have no proof yet: the proof is a hole, written sorry. Some holes are results that people already proved on paper. Other holes are open problems. I used both kinds.

Why I needed a separate checker

An AI that checks its own work can fake success, even without meaning to. It can leave a hole in the proof. It can add an assumption that it never proved. It can quietly change the question to an easier one.

So I used one rule: nothing the AI says counts. Only a separate checker decides.

How the checker works

  1. The AI works in its own GitHub repository. It can change anything there, so I trust nothing there.
  2. When the AI thinks it is done, it saves its work with a special message that starts with "submit".
  3. A second repository, the checker, finds that message. The AI cannot change the checker.
  4. The checker throws away the AI's copy of the question and uses its own frozen copy.
  5. A tool from the Lean team, Comparator, runs in a locked sandbox. It checks that the proof answers exactly the frozen question, uses only the three basic assumptions that all of normal math uses, and passes the kernel.
  6. The checker writes a green or red mark on the AI's work. If the mark is red, it also writes the reason.

The checker was private until the evening of 4 October. It is public now, because GitHub runs public repositories for free and my free minutes for private ones ran out. The checker does not need to be secret: it rejects a cheat even when the cheater knows its rules.

I tried to fool the checker first

Before the real runs, I gave the checker one correct proof and seven cheating ones. The correct proof passed. The checker rejected six of the cheats at once, each for the right reason.

The seventh cheat got through. It was a proof of an easier question that also printed the checker's own success message while it was built. The checker looked for that message in its log, and the fake proof had written it there itself. I changed the checker to read the real result instead of the log. That fake proof is now a permanent test.

How I know the frozen question is the right question

I am not a mathematician, so I cannot read a Lean claim and say that it matches the English problem. I use three checks instead.

  1. The frozen question is not typed by hand. It is built from the library's own claim, and the checker confirms by computer that the two are identical.
  2. The checker runs the definitions on small examples, and I compare the answers with the published ones.
  3. For each problem I get a small page where I can play the problem by hand on a small case.

Part 1: results that were already known

Erdős problem 105 (the warm-up)

  • The question. Take two separate groups of points in the plane, A and B. A has four more points than B, and A's points are not all on one line. Must some line through two A points miss every B point?
  • What was known. A person on the Erdős problems forum, Xichuan, found 10 points for A and 6 points for B where every line through two A points hits a B point. So the answer is no.
  • What the AI did. It wrote those 16 points into Lean. Then it proved in Lean that they meet every condition, and that all 45 lines hit B.

Green's problem 72 for small grids

  • The question. In an N by N grid, how many points can you place so that no three are on one line?
  • The easy part. You can never place more than 2N. The library already had a proof of that.
  • What was known. For every N from 3 to 60, computer searches found a set with exactly 2N points. They are in Flammenkamp's public database and in Prellberg's published table.
  • What the AI did. It took the 58 known point sets. It proved in Lean that each one really has no three points on a line; for N = 60 that is about 280,000 groups of three points. Then it proved the general step: one valid set of 2N points means 2N is the maximum.
  • The result. The claim "for every N from 3 to 60, the maximum is exactly 2N" now has a complete proof that the Lean kernel checks.

Does a manager AI help?

I compared two setups on Green's problem 72. Both used the same AI model, Claude Opus 5.5.

  • With harness. A manager AI gives the work to fresh helper AIs. It keeps a log of what each helper tried and gives the next helper the reason the last one failed.
  • Bare. One AI with a plain instruction and nothing else.

Two harder known results

I then picked results where the proof is an idea and not a computer check. Both are about Vizing's conjecture from 1963, which is still open. The conjecture is about guards on a network: how many guards a product of two networks needs, compared with the two networks alone.

  • Clark and Suen (2000) proved a weaker form of the conjecture. One bare AI session proved it in Lean. The checker accepted the first submission after 22 minutes. Cost: about 2 USD.
  • Suen and Tarr (2012) proved a slightly stronger form. One bare AI session proved it in Lean. The checker accepted the first submission after about 31 minutes. Cost: about 3 USD.

So short known proofs, one to three pages on paper, are also too easy to need a harness. That is why I moved to open problems.

Part 2: open problems

The method comes from Terence Tao

Terence Tao, one of the best-known living mathematicians, published two videos in 2026 where he uses Claude Code to write Lean proofs. I read both transcripts and used his recipe.

  1. Do not ask for the whole proof at once. His one-shot attempt ran 45 minutes and finished nothing. Step by step took about 25 minutes.
  2. Write the argument in plain words before any Lean.
  3. Build a skeleton first: state every step in Lean with a hole, make it compile, then fill the holes one at a time.
  4. Put the instructions in a file. Keep a second file of lessons that every session reads and updates.
  5. One side creates and the other side checks. The AI is safe for creating only where the checking keeps up.

He also says that he does not hand the AI a step that is mathematically hard. He expects it to fail there. That turned out to describe my results.

I took 16 claims that the library marks as open. Each got one AI session with a one-hour limit and four phases: understand the claim, try to break it by computer search, try to prove it, report. My mistake: "open" in the library did not mean open

Two sessions reported a full proof, and the checker accepted both. For a moment that looked like two solved open problems. It was not. Other people had proved both conjectures months earlier, and the library's "open" label was out of date.

My scan had read only that label. I then searched for earlier work on all 16 problems. Five of them were already settled: three proved and two disproved. Since then, every problem gets a search for earlier work before any credit goes to it.

Stage 2: ten attempts on each of two problems

I picked the two problems where stage 1 made the most progress and where I found no solution by anyone. Each got one long session. A manager AI (Claude Fable 5.1) kept a notebook and gave single steps to fresh helper AIs (Claude Opus 5.5). A second helper attacked every paper argument before it went into Lean. Each session stopped after ten attempts. Cost: 68 USD.

  • Catch-Up. Two players take turns to take numbers from 1 to N. The player who is behind keeps taking until they catch up. The claim: if 1 + 2 + ... + N is even, perfect play ends in a draw. The AI proved the claim in Lean for every N up to 16. It also proved that the full claim follows from four smaller statements. A computer check supports the four statements, but nobody has proved them.
  • Squares modulo n. The AI proved the claim in Lean for four kinds of n: even numbers, primes, powers of a prime, and a power of one prime times a second prime. So a counterexample, if one exists, has a more complex form. The smallest family that is still open is p²q². A computer search found no counterexample below 16 billion.

Both sets of Lean proofs build on a clean machine with no holes. They are partial results. Neither problem is solved.

Why the AI did not solve an open problem

It was not a lack of computer power, and it was not a lack of attempts.

  • The AI did the routine work fast: small cases, computer searches, reductions to simpler statements, and Lean proofs of all of those.
  • Each problem then stopped at one step that needs a new idea. For Catch-Up, ten attempts in a row ended at the same step.
  • More attempts gave more cases around that step. They did not give the idea.
  • Other teams report the same result. A public benchmark ran AI models on the same number-sequence problems, and those runs failed too.

This matches what Tao says in his videos. The AI is good at the steps you can check. The hard step still needs a person or a new method.

Part 3: what worked better, known results with no computer-checked proof

The search for earlier work showed a more useful kind of task. Some results are settled on paper, or by a computer search, but have no Lean proof. A Lean proof of such a result is new, small, and useful to the people who keep the library.

A disproof: TxGraffiti conjecture 3

A program called TxGraffiti made a conjecture about graphs where every point has the same number of neighbours. Other people found a graph with 48 points that breaks it, and published it with their own computer check. The library still listed the conjecture as open, with a request to fix it.

I gave the AI only the list of the graph's connections. In about 20 minutes it wrote a Lean proof that the graph breaks the conjecture. The longest part is a search with 124,512 steps, which the Lean kernel checks in 36 pieces. The checker accepted the first submission. I found no Lean proof of this disproof before the run. The graph is not mine: its authors found it.

The checker accepted 21 of the 23 proofs, each on its first submission. Two runs ended with a partial result. Most proofs took the AI less than 20 minutes.

I gave the AI no proof idea for three tasks, only the claim. It finished one of them, the first result on lists of triples, and got stuck on the other two. That fits the pattern from the open problems: the AI writes down a known argument fast, and it can get stuck where it has to find the argument.

These 23 were holes in the library, not open problems. Other people run AI on the same library, and I did not check every result for an earlier Lean proof somewhere else. At least two have one, as far as I can tell: R(4, 4) = 18 and the complexity of powers of 3.

What this is not

  • It is not a new discovery. No open problem was solved.
  • The partial results on Catch-Up and on squares modulo n are Lean proofs that build with no holes. The checker did not judge them, because the checker only judges a full frozen question. No mathematician has looked at them.
  • The graphs that break the two TxGraffiti conjectures were found by other people. My runs add the Lean proofs.
  • The last 23 results were holes in the library. Some have earlier Lean proofs elsewhere, and I did not check all of them.
  • My search for earlier work used the web and may have missed something.
  • Three runs per setup is a small sample for the harness comparison.
  • Claude wrote the checks that each frozen question matches the library. I checked them with small examples and playable pages, not by reading the Lean.
  • Nothing is posted yet to the Erdős problems site or to formal-conjectures.

In one line

AI agents turn known math into computer-checked proofs quickly and for a few dollars each. They did not solve an open problem: the missing piece there is an idea, and more attempts did not produce one.
